# 1,400 Free Signups, 25 Paid Seats: Which Number Measures AI Adoption?

In March 2026, DotAI -- my company -- co-hosted a free livestream with OpenClaw that drew over 1,400 registrations. Three months later, DotAI's AI Builders bootcamp filled twenty-five in-person seats at HK$24,500. Both numbers are mine, so discount what follows. But the gap between them matters, because most of the AI training industry is measuring the wrong one.

**The 1,400**

The livestream was a 25-minute walkthrough of what AI agents actually do when built properly -- real questions in the chat, genuine energy, the kind of event that produces a number worth putting on a slide. A hands-on class followed nine days later. Agent-building sessions and an alumni clinic ran through April. Weekly Academy classes continued into June. Each step asked more of participants -- more time, more effort, more willingness to sit with tools that don't cooperate on the first try. Each step had fewer people in the room.

That narrowing isn't failure. Curiosity at scale looks a lot like attention, and attention without structure fades by Monday. Most people who attend a free AI event return to their existing workflow by mid-week -- not because the content was poor, often it's excellent -- but because inspiration without a system to hold it decays at the rate it always has.

**The 25**

The bootcamp participants committed weeks of evenings to building something that might not work, in a room with no chat window, no replay button, no anonymity. Everyone visible, everyone accountable. That is not a scaled-down version of the free event. It is a different activity entirely.

After a year of running public classes alongside corporate engagements, the pattern is consistent enough to name. The industry celebrates reach because it's easy to count, easy to report, and fits on a slide. "We reached 1,400 people" belongs in a board update. "Twenty-five people showed up every week for a month and changed how they work" is harder to quantify and easier to overlook. But adoption -- the part where someone moves from knowing what AI is to using it differently in their actual work -- lives in the second sentence.

**Three questions worth asking before you buy AI training**

If you're evaluating AI training for your organisation, these separate awareness programs from adoption programs:

1. **What do participants produce during the session?** If the answer is "they watch a demo and ask questions," that's awareness -- useful, but don't expect Monday to look different. If they build something with the tools during the session, a workflow or prototype they take back to their desk, that's practice. Practice is where behavior change starts.

2. **What happens in week two?** A single session, no matter how good, fades at the rate attention always has. Ask whether there's a follow-up structure -- a second session, a check-in, anything connecting the training to participants' actual work after the event. Programs that change behavior have something in week two. Programs that produce good satisfaction scores don't need one.

3. **Can the vendor name five participants who changed a specific workflow?** Not "expressed interest" or "rated it highly" -- actually changed how they do a recurring task. If the evidence on offer is registration counts, satisfaction scores, and headcount reached, those are awareness metrics. Awareness is worth buying. Adoption is a different claim, and it needs different evidence.
